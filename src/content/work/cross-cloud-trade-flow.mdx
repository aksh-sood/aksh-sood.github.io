## Context

Trade flow for a global financial market infrastructure provider had to move between
systems that did not share an owner. Some of it ran on our EKS clusters. Some of it landed
in Kafka on Amazon MSK. Some of it had to reach services the client ran on Google Cloud.

This is the work I spend most of my time on now, and the reason it is on this site: almost
none of the difficulty is in the pipes. It is in the fact that one end of every integration
belongs to a team you do not manage, on a schedule you do not set, inside an account you
cannot read.

## Constraint

Two constraints shaped every decision.

The first was credentials. The obvious way to connect a workload in one cloud to a service
in another is a long-lived key in a secret, and for a customer with this audit posture that
is not an option — nor should it be. Static credentials in a settlement path are a finding
waiting to happen.

The second was the schema. FX trade payloads are not a shape you get to negotiate freely:
both ends have to agree exactly, the meaning of a field cannot drift, and a change on one
side cannot silently break the other. Whatever we chose had to carry that agreement
somewhere both teams could see it.

## What I did

For identity, I used each platform’s own federation rather than issuing anything.

Within AWS, workloads reach MSK through IRSA: the pod gets a service account, the service
account is trusted by an IAM role, and the broker authorises the role. No key is stored,
and access is scoped to the workload rather than to the cluster.

Reaching GCP is the same idea across a trust boundary. Workload Identity Federation lets
GCP accept the Kubernetes service account token our cluster already issues as proof of
identity, and exchange it for short-lived GCP credentials. The result is that a pod in our
EKS cluster can publish to the client’s Pub/Sub topic without either side holding a static
credential for the other. That property is what made the security review short.

For the payloads, Protobuf. The schema is the contract, it lives in version control, both
sides generate from it, and a field cannot change meaning without the change being visible
as a diff. On an integration where the other team’s release schedule is not mine to control,
having the contract be a reviewable artifact rather than a shared understanding is the
difference between a rollout and an incident.

The rest was the unglamorous part: joining their calls, walking through failure modes with
their engineers, and debugging live inside their environment when a message did not arrive.
A cross-cloud path has more places to be wrong than a single-cloud one, and most of them are
only visible from the other side.

## Outcome

Trade-flow data moves between systems the customer does not fully control, with no static
credentials anywhere in the path and a schema both teams can read.

What I took from it is narrower than the architecture. The design that wins these is almost
never the clever one — it is the one the other team can operate without me on the call. Both
halves here are each platform’s native federation and a schema format their engineers
already knew, which is why handing it over was uneventful.
