## Context

We ran a production Kubernetes fleet for financial infrastructure customers. It had grown
the way these things grow: a cluster per customer, sensible instance choices made two years
earlier, and resource requests copied from whatever the last service asked for. Nothing was
wrong. It was just expensive, and nobody could say precisely why.

Cost work on a platform like this has a trap in it. The workloads are settlement-adjacent,
so a saving that costs you headroom during a busy period is not a saving — it is a deferred
incident. Anything I did had to survive a traffic spike.

## Constraint

Two things narrowed the options.

The first was that I could not schedule downtime to do it. These are production clusters
serving customers who did not ask to participate in a cost exercise, so every change had to
be a rolling one that could be stopped halfway.

The second was that I did not trust the numbers we had. Resource requests in a fleet that
has grown organically tell you what somebody once typed, not what the workload uses. Before
changing anything I needed actual utilisation, which meant the observability work had to
come first.

## What I did

I went after the two costs separately, because they have different shapes.

The first is the price of a unit of compute. Graviton is materially cheaper per unit than
the equivalent x86 instance, so the win there is arithmetic — provided everything you run
has an arm64 image and nothing in the dependency chain quietly doesn’t. Most of that
migration was not Kubernetes work at all. It was finding the containers that only existed
as amd64, working out which ones had arm64 upstream and which needed rebuilding, and moving
them across without a flag day. Alongside it I right-sized what was left, using the
utilisation data rather than the requests.

The second cost is the gap between capacity you are paying for and capacity you are using,
and that one is structural. Cluster Autoscaler works from node groups you define in advance,
which means you are still the one guessing at instance shapes, and you are still paying for
the rounding error on every guess.

Karpenter removes the guess. It looks at the pods that cannot be scheduled and provisions
the instance that actually fits them, from a much wider pool of types, and consolidates
nodes back down when the workload shrinks. VPA fixed the requests themselves, so the pods
Karpenter was packing were asking for something close to what they used. KEDA handled the
workloads whose demand is driven by a queue rather than by CPU, so they scale on the thing
that actually predicts load.

The headroom question I answered by keeping it explicit rather than incidental. The old
fleet had slack because the sizing was loose — spare capacity nobody had decided on. The new
one has slack because consolidation is tuned to leave it. That is a smaller amount of
headroom, but it is a chosen amount, and it holds through a spike because it was sized
against observed spikes rather than against a guess.

## Outcome

The Graviton migration and right-sizing took $180,000 a year off the bill. Rebuilding
autoscaling on Karpenter, VPA and KEDA cut compute cost by a further 80%, and performance
held through traffic spikes.

The change I would actually defend is the third one, which does not appear on the invoice:
the fleet now reports what it uses. Before this, a question about capacity was a research
project. Now it is a dashboard, which is what made every subsequent cost conversation with
the client short.
