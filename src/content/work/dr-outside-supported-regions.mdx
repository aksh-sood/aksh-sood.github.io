## Context

A global financial market infrastructure provider settles foreign-exchange trades on our
platform. Settlement runs on messaging, and messaging is the part that cannot quietly
degrade — if a broker stops accepting messages, trades stop moving, and the people on the
other end notice within minutes rather than hours.

The client needed a disaster recovery position they could put in front of their own
auditors: a named passive region, a tested failover procedure, and a recovery objective
somebody had actually measured rather than estimated. Their regulatory footprint set the
geography. Oregon was primary. London and Ireland were where the data was allowed to go.

## Constraint

The managed message broker offers cross-region replication, which is the obvious answer and
the one I wanted to use. It only supports specific region pairs, and ours was not one of
them. The feature replicated between plenty of regions we had no permission to use, and not
between the three we did.

That left the usual bad options. We could have moved the workload to a supported pair, but
the region choice came from the client’s regulatory position and was not ours to change. We
could have accepted a longer recovery objective and restored from backups, but restoring a
broker from backup loses in-flight messages, and in-flight settlement messages are exactly
the ones that matter. We could have written our own replication on top of the broker, which
is a thing people do and then maintain forever.

## What I did

I stopped treating replication as the mechanism and treated reachability as the mechanism
instead.

ActiveMQ has had a network of brokers for a long time: brokers that know about each other
and forward messages across the link when a consumer is attached on the far side. It is not
a replication feature, so the managed region-pair restriction does not apply to it. It is
just brokers talking to brokers over a network I control.

So the topology became three brokers, one per region, joined as a network, with Oregon
active and the other two passive. Producers and consumers connect through a single endpoint
rather than to a broker directly, so a failover is a change in where that endpoint points
rather than a change every client application has to make. The whole thing is defined in
Terraform, which mattered more than it sounds: a DR topology that only exists because
somebody configured it by hand is a DR topology that drifts.

The part that took the longest was not building it. It was deciding what counted as
failed. Automatic failover on a network partition is how you end up with two regions both
believing they are primary, and for settlement messaging that is a worse outcome than being
down. So the failover is deliberate: monitored continuously, triggered by a human, and
executed by a procedure short enough that a human under pressure can follow it correctly.

## Outcome

We ran the failover rather than diagrammed it, and measured a recovery objective of ten
minutes from decision to traffic served from the passive region. That number went into the
client’s audit position because it came from an exercise, not an estimate.

The more useful result was quieter. Because the topology is ordinary broker networking
rather than a proprietary feature, the client’s engineers could read it, reason about it on
a call, and ask the kind of pointed questions that mean somebody actually understands their
own DR. A managed feature we didn’t control would have given us a shorter design document
and a much worse conversation.
