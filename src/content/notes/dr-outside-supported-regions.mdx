Last year I built multi-region disaster recovery for settlement messaging, and the managed
feature that was supposed to do it turned out not to work where we needed it. The
[case study](/work/dr-outside-supported-regions) covers what we shipped. This is the part
that generalises — the things I'd tell another engineer before they start.

## Check the capability matrix before you design around the feature

The managed broker offers cross-region replication. It is the obvious answer, it is
well documented, and I had already sketched the design around it before I read the
supported-region list closely enough.

It supports specific region pairs. Ours was not one of them. The feature replicated
happily between plenty of regions we had no permission to use, and not between the three
we did.

This is the mistake I'd most like back. Managed-service capabilities are not uniform
across geography, and the documentation usually presents the feature first and the matrix
second — a table further down the page, or a separate page entirely. If your region set is
driven by something you don't control, like a regulatory footprint, **read the matrix
before the feature description**. Ten minutes at the start would have saved a week of
designing something that could not be built.

The broader version: when a managed service is on the critical path of a design, find the
constraint page before you find the tutorial.

## When the abstraction doesn't fit, drop a level

Once replication was off the table, the options were all bad in familiar ways. Move the
workload to a supported region pair — except the geography came from the client's
regulatory position and was not ours to move. Accept a longer recovery objective and
restore from backups — except restoring a broker from backup loses in-flight messages, and
in settlement the in-flight messages are precisely the ones that matter. Write our own
replication on top of the broker — a thing people do, and then maintain forever.

What actually worked was changing the noun. I stopped looking for *replication* and
started looking for *reachability*.

ActiveMQ has had a network of brokers for a very long time: brokers that know about each
other and forward messages across the link when a consumer is attached on the far side.
It is not a replication feature. That is the whole point — the region-pair restriction is
a property of the managed replication feature, not of brokers talking to brokers over a
network I control.

The lesson I'd keep: a managed feature is a convenience wrapper over a primitive, and the
restrictions usually live in the wrapper. When the wrapper doesn't fit your case, the
primitive underneath is often unrestricted, older, better documented, and more boring. In
infrastructure, boring is a feature.

## Automatic failover is the wrong default for stateful messaging

This is where I spent the most time, and it was not build time — it was deciding what
counted as "failed".

The instinct is to automate the failover. For stateless services, sure. For a message
broker carrying settlement traffic, automatic failover on a network partition is how you
end up with two regions both believing they are primary. Two brokers accepting writes for
the same flow is a worse outcome than being down, because being down is obvious and
recoverable, and split-brain is neither.

So the failover is deliberate: monitored continuously, triggered by a human, executed by a
written procedure. And the procedure has to be short enough that a human under pressure at
an unreasonable hour can follow it correctly — which is a real design constraint on the
topology, not a documentation task you do afterwards.

If you take one thing: decide whether split-brain or downtime is worse for your data
*before* you decide whether failover is automatic. The answer determines the design.

## Measure the recovery objective by running it

A recovery objective you estimated is a number in a slide. A recovery objective you
measured is a number you can defend.

We ran the failover rather than diagramming it, and timed it from decision to traffic
served from the passive region. That gave us a real figure, and it went into the client's
audit position precisely because it came from an exercise.

Running it also surfaced things the diagram could not. The procedure had a step that read
clearly on paper and was ambiguous at 3am. You only find that by doing it.

## The design somebody else can read is worth more than the short one

The result I did not anticipate was the conversation.

Because the topology is ordinary broker networking rather than a proprietary feature, the
client's own engineers could read it, reason about it live on a call, and ask pointed
questions. That is what it looks like when somebody actually understands their own
disaster recovery position, and it is worth more than a shorter design document.

A managed feature we did not control would have given us less to explain and a much worse
conversation. When you are working inside somebody else's environment, the artifact you
leave behind is not the system — it's their ability to run it without you.

## And put it in code

Small one to finish on. The whole topology is defined in Terraform, which matters more than
it sounds. A DR topology that exists because somebody configured it by hand is a DR
topology that drifts, and the drift is invisible until the day you need it. The failover
test verifies the procedure; the code is what keeps the thing you tested and the thing you
have from diverging.
